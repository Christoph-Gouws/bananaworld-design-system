Option B — leave it as it is; the whole-line instruction wins

Today's behaviour, kept and written down as deliberate. An instruction given to the whole line overrides an instruction the author gave to one box the older way — quietly.

The buttons jump to the top with everything else. Whoever built the screen wrote “middle” and got “top”, with no warning of any kind. On a busy screen this is easy to miss and hard to explain.

2 · The order of who wins

1This one box, said the modern way — always wins
2The whole line — beats anything else said about a single box
3This one box, said the older way — ignored whenever the line has spoken
4Nothing said at all — sits in the middle, as it always has

The argument for it: the modern way of writing an instruction should outrank the older way. The argument against: the older way was the only way to say this until last week, so real screens are written that way.

What choosing this means

Nothing is built. The reviewer's point is answered by writing the rule down rather than changing it, and the piece of work becomes a note in the manual.

  • Cheapest. No change to how anything works, so no risk of breaking anything.
  • The catch: the first team to use the whole-line instruction will hit this, lose a few hours to it, and it will look like a fault in the shared kit — because it is one.
  • It also contradicts what was approved for this option a week ago, so the written record would need correcting.
